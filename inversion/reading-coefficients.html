    Reading the gravity coefficients#

    Last edited October 27, 2026

    An inversion estimates the densities of the components it is given; it does not say which components to give it. This page is the step before: how to read the gravity coefficients of an unknown body to decide which components to model, and where to start them. It derives what each kind of structure does to the coefficients, gives a procedure with the SBTK functions for each step, works an example, and reports how well the procedure finds known interiors (What the study found). The procedure applies to any body with a shape model; the examples use Didymos.

    The anomaly: what the interior adds#

    The coefficients of a body of constant density follow from its shape alone. The density structure is in the difference between the measured coefficients and those of the homogeneous body, the same shape with the bulk density ρ¯=M/V everywhere. Write the density as ρ(r)=ρ¯+δρ(r); since the mass is known, ∫δρdV=0. About a fixed point, the fully normalized coefficients are linear in the density, so the difference is

    ΔC¯lm=C¯lm−C¯lmhom=1MRl∫δρ(r)|r−rf|lY¯lm(r−rf)dV,

    the anomaly of each coefficient. SBTK expresses it about the center of figure rf, the homogeneous body's center of mass and a fixed point of the shape, so that the anomaly of a model is the sum of the anomalies of its components. Measurements about another point (a package about the origin of its shape-model file, or a field about the measured center of mass) are moved there exactly, with their covariance (Uncertainties and the expansion point). sbtk.interpret.anomaly computes it, for measurements or for a model:

    • an.value the anomaly, an.sigma its 1σ (the homogeneous field is exact), an.significance the ratio;
    • an.com_offset_km the degree-1 part as the center of mass minus the center of figure;
    • per degree, the RMS of the anomaly and of its 1σ (an.signal_rms, an.sigma_rms): a degree whose anomaly is not well above its 1σ carries no information about the interior;
    • the inertia rows of the measurements with the homogeneous body's values about the same point.

    sbtk.plot.significance draws every measurement with its ±1σ interval against the constant-density value, and counts those that differ from it by more than 1σ and 3σ. sbtk.interpret.summary prints the rest of this page's quantities.

    Signatures of components#

    A relative density Δρc in a component of volume Vc lowers the density of the rest of the body by ΔρcVc/V, because the mass is fixed. The anomaly it makes is Δρc times the component's signature

    sc=1M(Uc−VcVUbody),

    with U the integrals ∫(r/R)lY¯lmdV of the component and of the body about the center of figure (sbtk.gravity.component_integrals). The anomaly of any model is ∑cΔρcsc (checked to 10⁻¹² in testSignaturesAddUp). sbtk.interpret.signatures computes the signatures of candidate components, and fits each one alone to the measured anomaly: its density, the reduction of χ2 from the homogeneous body, and the correlation of its signature with the anomaly.

    What each kind of structure does#

    The homogeneous body's own coefficients set the scale. Each degree l≥2 of a field can be split into a scaled homogeneous field and the rest:

    C¯l·=klC¯l·hom+restl,kl=hl·clhl·hl,

    with hl and cl the 2l+1 coefficients of degree l of the homogeneous body and of the field (an.ratio, an.residual). Structure that is symmetric about the center of figure changes only kl; everything else shows in the rest.

    Structure Degree 1 Scaling kl The rest
    Dense core at the center of figure, mass excess qM 0 1−q at every degree none
    The same core moved by d qd/(3R) 1−q, to order (d/R)2 qpl(d), of order q(d/R)l
    Light core (q<0) qd/(3R) 1+|q| as above
    Surface layer, thickness t, relative density Δρ 0 1+Δρρ¯(s3−sl+3)≈1+Δρρ¯ltr¯ small
    Ring or torus about the z-axis, radius a 0 changes the even degrees J2,J4,… only
    The same, raised by h C10=qth/(3R) as above adds J3≈0.98(a/R)2C¯10
    Elongated core (ellipsoid) as a core as a core C¯22,S¯22: its elongation and direction
    Boulder of mass qbM at db qbdb/(3R) small every order, falling as (db/R)l

    A compact mass. A sphere inside the body is a point mass: its integrals are Vcp(d), with pl(d) the coefficients of a unit point mass at d from the center of figure (sbtk.internal.point_mass_sh). With q=ΔρcVc/M, the field is exactly

    cl=(1−q)hl+qpl(d)for every l≥1.

    At degree 1, h1=0 and p1(d)=d/(3R) (as C11,S11,C10): the center of mass moves by qd. At degree l the core's own term is of order q(d/R)l; for d→0 it vanishes and every degree is the homogeneous one scaled by 1−q. A dense core makes every coefficient smaller in the same proportion; a light core makes them larger.

    A surface layer. SBTK's layers are defined along rays from the center, so a layer from depth 0 to t is the body minus the body scaled by s=1−t/r along each ray (r the distance to the surface). In the cone of each facet the integrals of degree l scale with sl+3; for a layer thin compared with the radius of a nearly round body, s is about the same on every ray, the layer's signature is 1M(s3−sl+3)Ubody, and

    kl=1+Δρρ¯(s3−sl+3)≈1+Δρρ¯ltr¯.

    A light layer (Δρ<0) scales the coefficients down like a dense core, but more at higher degree: a ratio kl that falls with degree is a surface layer, a constant one a central mass.

    A ring or torus. A thin ring of mass qtM and radius a about the z-axis through the center of figure, at height h, has only zonal terms, C¯l0=qt(r/R)lPl(sinφ)/2l+1 with r2=a2+h2, sinφ=h/r. In the equatorial plane only the even degrees remain: P2(0)=−12, P4(0)=38, so a dense ring raises J2 and lowers J4, a light one does the opposite, and the sectoral and tesseral coefficients are untouched. Raised by h, the ring adds C¯10=qth/(3R) and C¯30≈−32qtha2/(7R3), so J3≈3327(a/R)2C¯10. A core moved by the same amount along z makes the same C¯10 with a J3 of order (d/R)3: J3 beside C10 tells a raised torus from a raised core.

    An elongated core has a degree-2 field of its own, C¯22 and S¯22 in proportion to the difference of its two equatorial axes; the angle 12atan2(S22,C22) of the rest of degree 2 is the direction of its long axis (or short axis, if it is light).

    A boulder far from the center makes degree 1 and a rest in every degree and order, falling as (db/R)l; its direction is the direction of the degree-1 anomaly when nothing else moves the center of mass.

    Degree 1 and the core#

    Degree 1 is the first moment of the density anomaly:

    M(rcm−rf)=∑cΔρcVc(r¯c−rf),

    with r¯c the centroid of component c. It fixes the product of a core's mass excess and its offset, not either alone: a dense core at d and a light core at −d give the same degree 1, and so does a lighter core farther out. The mass excess must come from elsewhere, and degree 2 gives it if the core is the only central mass. sbtk.interpret.central_mass solves the two equations of a single compact mass,

    qd=rcm−rf,c2−h2=q(p2(d)−h2),

    by iteration from q=1−k2 and d=(rcm−rf)/q, with their 1σ from the full covariance. For a sphere alone the answer is exact (testCentralMassOfOneSphere). With other structure, q is the net central condensation of all of it: a light surface layer adds to it, a light torus too, and d then comes out too short. That is why a model fit does not end the question of the core's position:

    • sbtk.invert.scan_position fits the densities with the core placed at each of a list of points, here along the measured center-of-mass offset, and returns the χ2 profile. It shows both sides of the ambiguity (a dense core on one side, a light one on the other) and how flat the valley between core mass and offset is;
    • Gauss–Newton on the core position (component.estimate_position), started from the lowest point of the profile, refines it with every coefficient at once;
    • sbtk.invert.recenter instead moves the core so that the model's center of mass equals the measured one at the fitted densities, s=M(rcm−rcmmodel)/(ΔρcVc), and repeated with new densities it is a fixed-point iteration. It converges in a few steps when the core is the only component that can move the center of mass; when other components can match degree 1 themselves, it stops where it starts (What the study found).

    Procedure#

    1. Read. an = sbtk.interpret.anomaly(obs, body, L) with every coefficient to degree L and the uncertainties you will use; sbtk.interpret.summary, sbtk.plot.significance. Note which degrees carry signal, whether the center of mass is off the center of figure, and the inertia rows.
    2. Scaling and rest. sbtk.plot.degree_ratio: kl below 1 at every degree is central mass, falling with degree a light surface, above 1 a light center or a dense surface. The rest, coefficient by coefficient (an.residual_significance): only even zonal terms point to a ring or torus about z; J3 with C10 to a raised torus; C22,S22 to an elongated center; every order in degrees 2–4 to something off center. The geometry (sbtk.interpret.geometry, sbtk.plot.anomaly_map): the center-of-mass offset as a direction; the principal axes of the second moment of degree 2 (C20, C21, S21, C22, S22), the axis mass was moved toward and the one it was moved away from, with their 1σ; and each degree as a map on the sphere with its directions of strongest excess and deficit. A compact mass gives one direction at every degree, a ring or belt the even degrees with extremes along the equator and at the poles. This is the reading that places components: a core along the offset, a torus about the axis degree 2 moved mass away from, a boulder where degrees 2–4 agree.
    3. One compact mass. sbtk.interpret.central_mass: the size and offset of the central mass if it were alone.
    4. Candidates. sbtk.interpret.signatures for the component types step 2 suggests, at several sizes and positions (sbtk.interior.sphere_grid for positions anywhere); sbtk.interpret.select adds them one at a time by the largest reduction of χ2, with an a priori 1σ of each density. It is a guide: similar signatures (a core and a surface layer) can share the anomaly between them.
    5. Model and core position. Set up the model (sbtk.invert.setup); sbtk.invert.scan_position along the center-of-mass offset; Gauss–Newton from the lowest point, with the geometry the rest points to free (torus height, ellipsoid axes).
    6. Test. The χ2 test (Goodness of fit); the anomaly of the fit against the measured one (sbtk.plot.anomaly); and a fit to the low degrees only, predicting the others (sbtk.invert.predict, sbtk.plot.coefficient_residuals; Fitting part of the coefficients).

    Example#

    examples/ex10_reading_coefficients.m runs the procedure on a hidden Didymos interior: a dense core (+500 kg/m³, radius 140 m) 30 m from the center of figure, a light torus (−400 kg/m³) 12 m above the equator and a light 15 m surface layer (−600 kg/m³), with coefficients to degree 5 about the origin of the shape-model file and 1σ close to the Hera radio-science values.

    MATLAB script: Reading the coefficients of a hidden interior
    run("/path/to/SBTK/sbtk_setup.m")
body = sbtk.body.create("Didymos", sbtk.shape.bundled("didymos"), 2600, 0.41);
cof  = body.centroid_km;
torus = sbtk.interior.torus("torus", 0.36, 0.05, 0, -400);
torus.center_km = cof + [0 0 0.012];
hidden = [sbtk.interior.sphere("core", cof + [0.025 -0.015 0.008], 0.14, +500), torus, ...
          sbtk.interior.layer("regolith", 0, 0.015, -600)];
exact = sbtk.data.simulate(body, sbtk.interior.create(hidden), 5, "reading example");
exact = sbtk.data.expand_about(sbtk.data.select_moi(exact, {}), [0 0 0]);
obs = sbtk.data.set_uncertainties(exact, sbtk.data.uncertainties([1.3e-5 2e-5 3e-5 3e-5 3e-5], 1, 0.02));

an = sbtk.interpret.anomaly(obs, body, 5);              % 1. read
sbtk.interpret.summary(an);
cm = sbtk.interpret.central_mass(an);                   % 3. one compact mass

model = [sbtk.interior.sphere("core", cof, 0.15, 0), sbtk.interior.torus("torus", 0.37, 0.055, 0, 0), ...
         sbtk.interior.layer("regolith", 0, 0.012, 0)];
model(2).center_km = cof;
opts = sbtk.invert.options(moi_labels={});
prob = sbtk.invert.setup(body, model, obs, opts);
u  = an.com_offset_km/norm(an.com_offset_km);           % 5. the core along the center-of-mass offset
sc = sbtk.invert.scan_position(prob, "core", cof + (-0.10:0.005:0.10)'*u);
model(1).center_km = sc.centers_km(sc.best, :);
model(1).estimate_position = true;
model(2).estimate_position = [false false true];
fit = sbtk.invert.solve(body, model, obs, opts);
sbtk.invert.summary(fit)
    Output: sbtk.interpret.summary(an)

    1. Read. 15 of the 35 coefficients differ from constant density by more than 1σ and 9 by more than 3σ. The center of mass is 0.31 m from the center of figure, measured to 1 cm: something is off center. J2 is 120σ below the homogeneous value and J4 30σ above it.

    2. Scaling and rest. Degree 2 is scaled down by 5.9%, degree 4 by 18%, degrees 3 and 5 by about 1–3%: the even degrees much more than the odd ones. A central mass alone would scale every degree alike and a surface layer would scale degree 4 more than degree 2 but degree 3 in between; the even–odd alternation is the signature of a ring about the z-axis. The rest confirms it: χ2 of 349 for the 4 degrees of freedom of degree 2 and 59 for the 8 of degree 4, against 26 and 13 for degrees 3 and 5, and in degree 2 most of it is J2. J3 (−4σ) and J5 (+3σ) point to a ring that is not in the equatorial plane.

    3. One compact mass. Degrees 1 and 2 as one mass give 5.9% of the mass, 4.4 m from the center of figure. The hidden core is 1.1% of the mass 30 m from it: the 5.9% is the net central condensation of the core, the light torus and the light surface layer together, and the offset divided by it comes out about 6 times too short (5 m instead of 30 m). On its own, a compact mass is the wrong reading here, as step 2 already said.

    5. Model and core position. The model has the three components step 2 points to, with the sizes a receiving team would guess (core radius 150 m, torus radii 370 and 55 m about the z-axis, a 12 m layer). The χ2 profile of the core along the center-of-mass offset has two valleys: a light core 45 m on the negative side (χ2≈160) and a dense core on the positive side, flat from 40 to 100 m (χ2≈93). The hidden core is at 27 m along this line.

    Gauss–Newton from the lowest point, with the torus height free, ends at a reduced χ2 of 0.65 (p=0.92): the model is consistent with the measurements. It puts the torus 12.2 m above the equator (hidden: 12 m, ±1.9 m) with a relative density of −359 ± 15 kg/m³ (hidden: −400). The core, however, has +122 ± 22 kg/m³ at 127 m from the center of figure, and the layer −1418 ± 184 kg/m³: a weak core far out and a very light surface explain the measurements as well as the hidden dense core and lighter surface, within the 1σ of the measurements and with this model's slightly wrong sizes. With the hidden sizes the fit recovers every density exactly (χ2=6×10−12). Degree 1 fixes the core's first moment; how it splits into mass and offset depends on the degree-2 scaling, which the layer shares. The position 1σ of Gauss–Newton (18 m) describes the valley near the solution, not the length of the valley: read the χ2 profile.

    6. Test. Fitted to degrees 1–3 only, the same model predicts the 16 coefficients of degrees 4 and 5 with a χ2 of 1.2 per coefficient: the model passes a test on coefficients it was not fitted to. The test does not separate the core from the layer either: at this precision degrees 4 and 5 do not tell the two explanations apart.

    What the study found#

    tests/studies/coefficient_reading_study.m applies the procedure to 110 random Didymos interiors whose answers are known, and tests/studies/coefficient_reading_figures.m summarizes it. Each true interior gives the exact coefficients to degree 5 about the origin of the shape-model file, weighted by the Hera radio-science 1σ (hlp6_uncertainties.m), and the same with one realization of noise. The interiors come in four families:

    Family True models Contents (random)
    no core 20 a torus about the z-axis (ring radius 300–400 m, tube 35–65 m, ±150 to ±700 kg/m³, up to 25 m above or below the equator) and a light surface layer (8–30 m, −200 to −1100 kg/m³)
    core 30 one sphere (radius 100–200 m, +200 to +900 kg/m³, or −200 to −700 in one case of four) up to 60 m from the center of figure
    core, torus, layer 30 the three together
    with unmodeled structure 30 the three, the torus tilted up to 4°, 1 to 3 dense boulders (25–50 m) and a Gaussian random field

    Each data set is fitted with 8 sets of coefficients, from degree 1 and J2 to every coefficient to degree 5, with two models of fixed sizes, the sizes a team would have to guess: a core of radius 150 m, and that core with a torus (radii 370 and 55 m) and a 12 m layer. The core is placed by up to five procedures: at the center of figure; Gauss–Newton from there; the χ2 profile along the center-of-mass offset, then Gauss–Newton (also with the torus height free); the fixed-point iteration of sbtk.invert.recenter. That is 15,840 fits; on the 49,152-facet model a fit takes 2 s (core fixed) to 8 s (profile and Gauss–Newton), 50 s with the torus height free, and the study 3 hours on 10 workers.

    Reading without a fit#

    • A core alone scales every degree by the same factor (kl=0.992, the median 1−q), leaves no rest, and sbtk.interpret.central_mass returns its mass excess and position exactly in all 30 models.
    • A torus and a layer scale degree 4 down most (median k4=0.89, against k2=0.96 and k3=0.96) and leave a rest in degrees 2 and 4 (median χ2 108 and 154) much larger than in degrees 3 and 5 (9 and 6): the signature of the even degrees. The compact mass from degrees 1 and 2 is then the net central condensation: it converges in 90% of the models, its mass is off by a factor (median 1.25 of the core's, with 10% of the models below −2.9 and 10% above 9.3) and its position 20 m (median) from the core.
    • Boulders and a random field add a rest in the odd degrees: median χ2 84 in degree 3 and 24 in degree 5, against 9 and 6 without them. A rest in the odd degrees is the sign that a core, a torus and a layer will not be enough.

    Degree 1 and the core#

    • Degree 1 and J2 determine a core that is alone. The four numbers fix its four unknowns: without noise the core is recovered exactly; at the Hera precision it is 4.6 m from the true core (median), within 10 m in 83% of the models, with its mass excess within 2%. More coefficients do not move it closer (4.1–4.6 m with every set to degree 5): the position is limited by how well degree 1 and J2 are measured relative to the core's mass. Gauss–Newton, the profile and the fixed-point iteration give the same result.
    • Matching degree 1 by moving the core is an iteration that converges when the core is the only component that moves the center of mass. With the core model, sbtk.invert.recenter converges in all 240 fits with noise, in 3 steps (median). With the three-component model, fitted to degree 1 and J2, it stops at its first step with the core at the center of figure: the torus and the layer, cut by the irregular surface, have their own degree 1, and their fitted densities already match the measured one. With more coefficients it converges in 53–77% of the fits, after 6–32 steps, and ends 15–30 m from the core.
    • Gauss–Newton from the center of figure with the three-component model finds a lone core within 10 m in 17% of the models with degree 1 and J2 (its mass excess wrong by about 300%); from the lowest point of the χ2 profile, in 83%.
    • The χ2 profile chooses the side. Its lowest point is on the side of the true core in every model of a lone core without noise and in 93% with noise; with a torus and a layer in 79%, with unmodeled structure in 80%. Most profiles (64–81%) have two valleys, a dense core on one side and a light one on the other.

    Which coefficients#

    With the right model (a lone core fitted with the core model) every set predicts the coefficients it was not fitted to with χ2 of 1.0 per coefficient, as the noise: degree 1 and J2 are enough. With a torus and a layer, the three-component model of guessed sizes, placed by the profile and Gauss–Newton, with noise:

    Without noise the same model places the core within 10 m in 53–60% of the models from degrees 1–4 or 1–5 (median 8–10 m).

    • J4 is the coefficient that separates a torus and a layer from a core. Added to degree 1 and J2, it lowers the χ2 of the other coefficients from 21.5 to 3.8 per coefficient and the error of the core's mass excess from 151% to 53%. J3 adds little: at this precision it is mostly noise, and fitting it moves the core to fit the noise (47 m).
    • A fit to degrees 1–2 can be consistent and wrong. Degree 2 has five numbers; 63% of these fits pass the χ2 test and still predict the other coefficients at 16 per coefficient. For interiors without any core the same model passes on degrees 1–2 in 60% of the models and predicts the rest at 45. Test a model on coefficients it was not fitted to (Fitting part of the coefficients).
    • The low degrees predict the high ones once J4 is in: fitted to degrees 1–4, the model predicts degree 5 at 1.5 per coefficient.

    What limits the finding#

    • The sizes of the components. The models' sizes are fixed and the true ones random. With the true sizes the fit recovers every density exactly (the Example); with guessed sizes the densities and the core position absorb the difference, and the core is found within 10 m in about a third of the models with noise and the mass excess to 50%. Search the sizes as well as the densities (Searching the geometry), as in ROB-DATA3.
    • Structure the model does not have. With boulders and a random field the three-component model places the core within 10 m in at most a fifth of the fits, and is consistent with the coefficients in at most 7% of the fits to degrees 1–2 + J3 + J4 or 1–3 and in none of the fits to degrees 1–4 or 1–5. The rest in the odd degrees says so before any fit.
    • Noise costs about a factor two in the core position with three components (8–10 m without noise, 14–15 m with).

    In practice#

    1. If every degree is scaled alike and there is no rest, the interior is a core: fit a core to degree 1 and J2, and test it on the rest.
    2. If degree 4 is scaled down more than degree 2 and the rest is in the even degrees, add a torus and a layer, and fit at least degree 1, J2 and J4; degrees 1–4 when the low degrees are not enough.
    3. If there is a rest in the odd degrees, three components are not enough; do not trust a core position from them.
    4. Place the core by its χ2 profile, not by Gauss–Newton from the center of figure; the fixed-point iteration only for a core that is alone.
    5. Vary the sizes of the components, and add compact masses only where the measurements ask for them (Searching the geometry).
    6. Accept a model only if it predicts coefficients it was not fitted to.
